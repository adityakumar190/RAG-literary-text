Abstract

This project uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to extract the flora and fauna named in six English-language books of Indian literature available on Project Gutenberg. For each book, one list of flora and one list of fauna was produced and stored as Python lists, giving 117 entries in total (70 flora and 47 fauna). The lists were then read and examined. Mangoes, palms, lotus, jasmine, nightingales and crows recur across several books. Many entries are plausible names of plants and animals, but 27 entries (23%) are placed in the wrong list or are not plants or animals at all, 42 entries carry a note from the model, and the fauna list of one book omits many animals that the poems name. The extracted lists are therefore a useful first draft that needs human review, and precision and recall were not measured against a manual annotation.

1Introduction

Literary texts from and about India refer constantly to plants and animals: the trees under which a scene is set, the birds heard at dusk, the flowers worn or offered. These references are part of the cultural setting of a text, and collecting them is one way of extracting Indian cultural elements from literature. This project takes flora and fauna as a first, concrete category of such elements.

The task is done with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), in which a language model writes its answer from passages that a retrieval step has selected from a source, not from memory alone (Lewis et al., 2020). RAG suits this task because a whole book is long, the plants and animals are scattered across it, and every entry can in principle be traced to a passage of the text.

The six books differ in form and period. They include ballads and poems, letters, a novel and a play, and two of them (the letters and the novel) were translated into English from Bengali and one (the play) from Sanskrit. Together they test the method on varied material. The project asks how well the method works and what kinds of mistake it makes.

Research question: How reliably can retrieval-augmented generation extract Indian flora and fauna from Indian literary texts, and what kinds of errors does it make?

2Objectives

  1. To select six Indian literary texts of different forms from Project Gutenberg.
  2. To extract the flora and the fauna named in each text using RAG.
  3. To store the results as separate Python lists for each book and each category.
  4. To examine the lists: their size, the elements that recur across books, and the kinds of error they contain.
  5. To answer the research question and state what must be reviewed before the lists are reused.
  6. To publish the lists in a public GitHub repository and present the work on a webpage hosted through GitHub Pages.

4Method

The work was carried out in five steps.

  1. Selection of texts: Six books of Indian literature were chosen from Project Gutenberg to cover different forms (verse, letters, a novel and a play) and both original and translated English.
  2. Separate sources: Each book was treated as a separate source, so that the entries for one book come only from that book.
  3. Retrieval and generation: For each book, passages that mention plants and animals were retrieved, and a language model was asked to list the flora and the fauna named in them. The model often added a short note in brackets to an entry, for example to say that an animal is only implied by the passage.
  4. Storage: The entries were saved as two Python lists per book (twelve lists in all) in the file flora_fauna.py, named <book>_flora_RAG_extraction and <book>_fauna_RAG_extraction.
  5. Examination: The lists were counted and read against the books. Entries that are not plants or animals, entries repeated within or across the two lists of a book, and entries that carry a note were identified, and the elements that recur across books were collected.

      8Discussion

      8.1 What the extraction gets right

      Where a text names plants and animals plainly, the extraction follows it closely. In Ancient Ballads and Legends of Hindustan, the poem “Buttoo” describes a forest with a stanza of trees: the betel-nut, the tamarind, the neem, the seemul, the Indian fig, the bamboo, the mangoe, the sirish, the peepul and the bramble, with bulbuls, rooks and crows among them. All of these appear in the extracted lists. The elements that recur across books in Table 4 (mango, palm, rose, lotus, jasmine, the nightingale and the crow) point to a shared stock of plants and birds across authors and forms, although each entry still needs to be checked against its passage.

      8.2 What goes wrong

      The errors fall into four kinds, as Table 3 shows.

      • Animals under flora. In The Poison Tree the flora list begins with the title tree and then gives musk-rats, bats, mice, pigeons and sparrows, which also appear in its fauna list. In Sakoontala the flora list includes the crow, the peacock and the koil, which are also in its fauna list.
      • Things that are not plants under flora. “Examiner” comes from the introductory memoir of Ancient Ballads, where it is the name of a newspaper. “Kincob” is a gold brocade fabric, but the model described it as a fruit or meat. Wells, harvest, the night-wind, the sky and the stars are not plants.
      • People and abstractions under fauna. Humans, eunuchs, “Indian”, Dravidians, inhabitants and population are people, and Time’s river, Surya, Varuna and Prithvi are natural or divine entities.
      • Misreadings of context. “Bulls” was extracted from the line about “the mangoe-tope, a close dark ring, home of the rooks and clamorous crows”, which does not mention bulls. “Sirish (Ashoka Tree)” treats two different trees as one.

      8.3 Differences between books

      The share of entries marked * is lowest in Ancient Ballads (2 of 28 entries, 7%), whose poems often name trees and animals directly, and highest in Tales of Ind (5 of 8 entries, 63%), which has the shortest lists. With so few entries the percentage for Tales of Ind is unstable, but it is consistent with the method being less reliable when a text mentions plants and animals only indirectly, as the entries for this book (palm-groves, rice-fields, a village, its population) suggest. In the other four books, between 23% and 26% of the entries are marked *.

      8.4 The model’s notes

      Of the 117 entries, 42 carry a note in brackets, and 16 of these notes say that the item is implied, likely, general, unspecified or metaphorical. By the model’s own account, such entries are inferences and not names found in the text, so they should be checked against the passage before they are accepted.

      8.5 What is missing

      Table 3 measures only entries that should not be in the lists. It says nothing about entries that are missing. Reading Ancient Ballads shows that the extraction is also incomplete: the poems name deer, peacocks, monkeys, tigers, jackals, elephants and cobras, but none of them is in its fauna list, and the saul and the champac are named in the tree stanza but are not in its flora list. The extraction therefore returns only part of what the book contains.

      8.6 Answer to the research question

      RAG extracts many of the plants and animals that a text names directly, and the lists contain good material, including the elements that recur across the six books. It is not reliable enough to be used without review. About one entry in four is in the wrong list or is not a plant or an animal, some entries are the model’s own inferences, and the lists omit animals and plants that the books name. The errors come from category confusion, from misreading the context of a passage and from stretching a mention into an entry. A reviewer should therefore check every entry against its passage and add what is missing.

      10Limitations

      • The lists were not compared with a manual annotation, so precision and recall are not reported. Table 3 counts entries that appear not to belong to their list, and the omissions in Section 8.5 were checked in one book only.
      • The classification of problem entries in Table 3 was made by reading the lists against the books, and a different reader might classify a few entries differently.
      • Names are not normalised. The same plant or animal appears in several forms (for example “Nightingale”, “Nightingales” and “Koil (Nightingale)”), and the counts treat each entry as given.
      • Entries are names only. The lists do not give the passage, the page or the meaning of the plant or animal in the text, and therefore cannot yet show how the cultural elements are used.
      • Three of the books are translations, so some names reflect the translator’s choices and spellings (for example “Sakoontala” and “Koïl”).
      • Six books are a small sample of Indian literature, and a larger corpus could give different results.

      11Conclusion

      This project used RAG to extract Indian flora and fauna from six Indian literary texts and produced twelve lists with 117 entries. The lists capture many plants and animals that the texts name directly and show elements that recur across books, such as the mango, the palm, the lotus, the nightingale and the crow. They also contain mistakes: 27 entries (23%) do not appear to belong to their list, 42 entries carry notes, and the fauna list of Ancient Ballads leaves out many animals that the poems name.

      The lists are therefore best used as a first draft. A next step is to check every entry against its passage, remove the entries that are not plants or animals, add the missing ones, normalise the names and record the passage for each entry. That would also allow precision and recall to be measured, and would extend the work from flora and fauna to other Indian cultural elements.
